# 4 Secrets that may change your life
Developing these four traits together creates a powerful feedback loop. When you generate better ideas, you have more options to adapt. When you are high agency, you act on those options. When you are resilient, you survive the inevitable failures of trying new things.
Here is a practical framework to build each of these habits into your daily life.

1. Become High Agency
High agency is the refusal to accept passive circumstances as final. Low-agency people look at a problem and ask, `Why is this happening to me?` High-agency people ask, `How do I get around this?`

* Default to action over permission: Stop waiting for someone to assign you a task or tell you the **right** way to do something. Start building, writing, or testing first, and ask for feedback on something tangible.
* Bypass the front door: When you want a job, a partnership, or an answer, don't just fill out the standard form. Find the person actually making the decision and send them a short, thoughtful message showing you've already done some of the work.
* Audit your language: Catch yourself when you say `I have to,` `I can't,` or `They won't let me.` Replace those phrases with **I choose to,** **I haven't figured out how to yet**, or **How can I convince them?**
* Solve the whole problem: If you hit a roadblock on a task, don't just report the roadblock to someone else. Bring the problem along with two or three proposed solutions.

2. Get Good at Generating Ideas
Idea generation is a volume game, not a lightning strike. The best way to have a great idea is to have dozens of bad ones first.

* Write 10 ideas a day: Every morning, pick a prompt (e.g., "10 ways to save an hour a week," "10 business ideas for pet owners," "10 ways to improve my current project") and force yourself to write down 10 answers. Numbers 1 through 6 will be obvious. Numbers 7 through 10 are where your brain stretches and finds original connections.
* Combine unrelated domains: Innovation rarely comes from inventing something from scratch. It comes from taking a solution from Industry A and applying it to Industry B. Read widely outside your primary field.
* Keep an "Friction Log": Carry a small notebook or keep a phone note dedicated entirely to minor annoyances you experience during the day. Every complaint or clunky process is a raw material for a new idea.
* Ask "What if the opposite were true?": Take a standard assumption in your life or work and flip it. If everyone is trying to make something faster and cheaper, ask what happens if you make it slow and expensive.

3. Be Very Resilient
Resilience is not about avoiding pain or stress; it is about shortening your recovery time.

* Separate identity from outcomes: When a project fails or you get rejected, view it as data, not a verdict on your worth. You didn't fail; an experiment produced an unexpected result.
* Practice voluntary discomfort: Regularly do hard things on purpose so that unexpected hardship feels familiar. Cold showers, intense workouts, fasting, or having difficult conversations train your nervous system to stay calm under pressure.
* Run a "Pre-Mortem": Before starting something important, imagine it is six months in the future and the project has completely failed. Write down all the reasons why. By anticipating the worst-case scenarios early, you remove their shock value and can build safeguards now.
* Focus strictly on the next micro-step: When things go wrong, looking at the whole mountain causes paralysis. Shrink your time horizon. Ask yourself, "What is the single most useful thing I can do in the next 15 minutes?" and only do that.

5. Be Adaptable to a Rapidly Changing World
Adaptability requires holding your skills, beliefs, and plans loosely. What worked five years ago may actively hold you back today.

* Learn how to learn fast: Instead of memorizing static facts, get good at rapidly deconstructing new tools and fields. Use the "80/20 rule" to identify the 20% of fundamentals in a new technology or skill that will give you 80% of the results, and learn those in a weekend.
* Build "T-shaped" skills: Have deep expertise in one or two core areas (the vertical bar of the T), paired with a broad, working familiarity across many adjacent areas like AI, basic finance, psychology, and communication (the horizontal bar).
* Update your beliefs publicly and easily: Treat your opinions like software—always in beta. When new evidence contradicts what you used to believe, say, "I used to think X, but now I see Y," without ego.
* Run small, cheap experiments: Never bet the farm on a single rigid five-year plan. Instead, treat your career and life like a series of two-week or three-month experiments. Keep what works, kill what doesn't, and pivot quickly.

## How to Start Today
Don't try to overhaul your entire personality overnight. Pick one micro-habit to run for the next seven days:

1. Write down 10 ideas every morning before checking your phone.
2. Fix one annoying thing in your environment or workflow without asking for help.
3. Try one new tool or workflow you've been putting off learning.
